## 8.3 Interpretation of the Five-Model Evaluation

The five-model comparison (Chapter 7, Table 7.2) is this project's central empirical result, and its most important interpretive lesson is negative: **the evaluation demonstrates that no single model dominates.** XGBoost's Macro F1 (0.7874) is the highest of the five, but by a narrow margin over Linear SVM (0.7789) and CodeBERT (0.7780) — a spread of less than 0.01 across the top three models. CodeBERT's accuracy (0.7976) is the highest of the five, yet its Macro F1 is third. SVM's FNR (0.1828) is the lowest of the five by a clear margin over XGBoost's (0.2581), a gap of over seven percentage points that is not marginal for a security-relevant metric.

**Figure 8.1. Security Error Rates by Approach.** *(figure_8_1_fnr_fpr_comparison.png)*

Figure 8.1 (`security_selection/plots/fnr_fpr_comparison.png`) makes this trade-off directly visible for the two models most central to this project's production decision: SVM's bars show a more balanced FNR/FPR profile, while XGBoost's bars show a visibly lower FPR at the cost of a visibly higher FNR. This is not an artefact of visualisation choice; it is the same trade-off reported numerically in Table 7.2, and this chapter treats it as a genuine, evidence-based tension rather than resolving it by simply declaring one model "better." The two right-hand bars in the same figure, showing the Decision Engine's auto-decided and all-case error rates, are discussed separately in Section 8.8 and should not be read as part of the five-model comparison itself.

**Table 8.2. Five-Model Performance Interpretation.**
| Model | Strongest on | Weakest on | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Linear SVM | FNR (0.1828), overall vulnerability recall | FPR (0.2000, joint-highest with RF) | Best at not missing a real vulnerability; produces more false alarms in exchange |
| Random Forest | — (no leading metric among the five in Table 7.2) | Macro F1 (0.7519, lowest) | Consistently mid-to-lower-ranked across every metric measured; not competitive with the other four on this evaluation |
| XGBoost | Macro F1 (0.7874), FPR (0.1467) | FNR (0.2581, highest of the five) | Best balanced overall classifier and most conservative on false alarms; trades this for the weakest miss-rate control |
| LSTM | — (no leading metric) | Macro F1 (0.7448, lowest), FNR (0.2688, highest) | Did not outperform the classical models on this dataset on any measured axis |
| CodeBERT | Accuracy (0.7976) | — | Competitive across the board and strongest on raw accuracy, but not the Macro F1 or FNR leader |

The evaluation indicates that model selection for a security-classification task is inherently multi-objective: a model optimised for Macro F1 is not automatically the model that misses the fewest real vulnerabilities, and a model with the highest raw accuracy is not automatically the model with the best-balanced per-class performance. This finding directly motivates why Chapter 1's research objectives called for a comparative evaluation across five models rather than assuming in advance which algorithm family would be strongest, and it is returned to directly in Section 8.4's discussion of the production selection itself.

## 8.4 XGBoost as the Production Model

XGBoost's selection as the production model is a multi-factor engineering and research decision, not a claim of universal metric superiority, and this chapter states that distinction explicitly because the evidence in Section 8.3 would not support the stronger claim. The decision rests on ten combined factors, each independently verifiable against Chapter 6 and Chapter 7: (1) the highest Macro F1 among the five models evaluated (0.7874); (2) the lowest false-positive rate (0.1467), meaning fewer safe code samples are incorrectly flagged, which matters directly for developer trust in a real-time tool; (3) the strongest CWE-89 F1 among the five (0.7931, Section 8.5); (4) use of the project's shared 7,610-dimensional hybrid feature representation, combining learned lexical features with 23 deterministic security/data-flow features; (5) real SHAP explainability already computed against the exact production booster, not an approximation; (6) established integration with the FastAPI backend's `predict_code()` pipeline; (7) established integration with the IntelliJ/Android Studio plugin's real-time annotation and Quick Fix workflow; (8) established integration with the Hybrid Decision Engine, whose evidence weights and calibrated thresholds were tuned against this specific model's output; (9) a practically deployable, low-latency inference cost (Chapter 7, Section 7.15: approximately 0.40 ms per prediction, the smallest single contributor to the total pipeline latency); and (10) an already-verified, reproducible production artefact with content-hash versioning (Chapter 6, Section 6.6).

This chapter explicitly acknowledges what this decision costs: **SVM's FNR (0.1828) is materially lower than XGBoost's (0.2581)** — a security-relevant model, evaluated purely on its ability to avoid missing a real vulnerability, would favour SVM over the production choice on this specific dataset and split. This is not concealed or reframed as a wash; the Decision Engine's role, discussed in Section 8.8, is precisely to compensate for this gap by combining XGBoost's prediction with independently-computed corroborating evidence, so that the deployed system's effective miss-rate at its production operating point (3.03% among auto-decided cases) is substantially lower than XGBoost's own raw FNR — but this compensation is a property of the combined system, not of the underlying classifier considered alone, and this chapter does not claim otherwise. LSTM and CodeBERT remain research comparison models throughout this discussion: neither is loaded by the production backend, and their evaluation — genuinely competitive in CodeBERT's case, genuinely uncompetitive in LSTM's — is discussed on its own terms in Section 8.7 rather than folded into the production justification.

## 8.5 CWE-89 (SQL Injection) Findings

The CWE-89-specific comparison (Chapter 7, Table 7.3) tells a partially different story from the aggregate five-model comparison in Section 8.3. **XGBoost achieved the strongest CWE-89 F1 (0.7931)** among the five models, a genuinely balanced result (its CWE-89 precision and recall are numerically identical). **CodeBERT achieved the strongest CWE-89 recall (0.8621) and the lowest CWE-89 FNR (0.1379)** of any model evaluated — this is the corrected, verified result, and it is the opposite of an earlier, disputed claim about CodeBERT's CWE-89 performance that this thesis explicitly does not use; the figure reported here (0.8621 recall, drawn from `research_eval_artifacts/codebert/test_metrics.json`'s per-class output) was independently verified against the stored artefact before being reported in Chapter 7 and is repeated unchanged here.

**Table 8.3. CWE-89 (SQL Injection) Findings.**
| Model | Recall | FNR | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Linear SVM | 0.8276 | 0.1724 | Second-strongest recall; competitive miss-rate control |
| Random Forest | 0.7931 | 0.2069 | Mid-ranked; matches XGBoost's recall but not its precision |
| XGBoost | 0.7931 | 0.2069 | Strongest F1 (0.7931); balanced precision/recall |
| LSTM | 0.5862 | 0.4138 | **Weakest recall of the five; comparatively high miss-rate for this specific vulnerability class** |
| CodeBERT | 0.8621 | 0.1379 | **Strongest recall and lowest FNR of the five** |

The evaluation indicates that CodeBERT's pretrained code representation was particularly effective at recognising the CWE-89 pattern specifically, even though its overall Macro F1 across both vulnerability classes (Section 8.3) did not lead the comparison — a finding this chapter reports precisely because it illustrates why an aggregate metric can obscure a per-vulnerability-class strength. LSTM's comparatively weak CWE-89 recall (0.5862, missing over 41% of real SQL-Injection cases in this evaluation) is a concrete instance of the pattern the literature review anticipated: Chakraborty et al.'s (2022) empirical finding that deep-learning vulnerability detectors do not reliably generalise as well as their architecture might suggest is directly reflected here, on this specific vulnerability class, in this specific dataset. This finding is not generalised beyond the evaluated dataset and split; a larger or differently-composed SQL-Injection training corpus could plausibly change LSTM's relative standing, and this chapter does not claim otherwise.

The direct security implication of this table is that a false negative on CWE-89 specifically means a real, exploitable SQL-Injection pattern that neither the plugin's real-time annotation nor the mobile application's risk assessment would surface to the person relying on the tool. This is why FNR, not accuracy or F1 alone, is treated as the primary lens for this per-CWE discussion, consistent with the framing already established in Chapter 7.

## 8.6 CWE-295 (Certificate Validation) Findings

The CWE-295-specific comparison (Chapter 7, Table 7.4) shows a materially different pattern from CWE-89, and this chapter treats that difference as itself an important finding rather than noise. **Linear SVM achieved the strongest CWE-295 F1 (0.8525) and a strong recall (0.8125)**, outperforming XGBoost on this specific vulnerability class. **XGBoost's CWE-295 recall (0.7188) is the weakest of the five models**, giving it the highest CWE-295 FNR (0.2812) — the same model that led CWE-89 F1 in Section 8.5 is the weakest performer on CWE-295 recall here.

**Table 8.4. CWE-295 (Certificate Validation) Findings.**
| Model | Recall | FNR | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Linear SVM | 0.8125 | 0.1875 | **Strongest F1 (0.8525) and strong recall for this class** |
| Random Forest | 0.7500 | 0.2500 | Mid-ranked |
| XGBoost | 0.7188 | 0.2812 | **Weakest recall of the five for this specific class** |
| LSTM | 0.7969 | 0.2031 | Second-strongest recall; competitive with SVM on this class specifically |
| CodeBERT | 0.7344 | 0.2656 | Highest precision (0.9400) but not the strongest recall or F1 |

The evaluation indicates that XGBoost remained competitive on CWE-295 without being the strongest model for this particular vulnerability class — its production selection (Section 8.4) is not undermined by this finding, since that selection was explicitly justified on combined evidence rather than per-CWE dominance, but this chapter states the specific limitation plainly: **a developer or user relying on the production model alone, without the Decision Engine's corroborating certificate-validation-specific evidence, would experience a higher real-world miss rate on CWE-295 than on CWE-89.** This is precisely why the Decision Engine weights confirmed certificate-validation evidence heavily (Chapter 5/6/7's documented `+2.0` weight for a confirmed vulnerable certificate check), a design choice this chapter now recognises as partially compensating for a real, measured weakness in the underlying classifier's CWE-295 recall specifically.

Taken together, Sections 8.5 and 8.6 support a conclusion this thesis states explicitly rather than leaving implicit: **no model, including the production model, is simultaneously the strongest performer on both CWE-89 and CWE-295 recall.** This chapter does not claim either vulnerability class is universally easier to detect; the relative difficulty visibly depends on which model and which metric is examined, and this finding is scoped to the evaluated dataset and split rather than presented as a general property of either vulnerability category.

## 8.7 Classical Machine Learning vs Deep Learning

The comparison between classical machine learning (SVM, Random Forest, XGBoost) and the two deep-learning approaches evaluated (LSTM, CodeBERT) does not support a simple, general claim in either direction, and this chapter deliberately avoids one. **Neither LSTM nor CodeBERT outperformed XGBoost in Macro F1** on this dataset: XGBoost's 0.7874 exceeds CodeBERT's 0.7780 and LSTM's 0.7448. LSTM's result is the clearer of the two: its Macro F1 is the lowest of all five models evaluated, and its FNR the highest, indicating that a sequence model trained entirely from scratch on this project's dataset scale did not out-detect models built on the hybrid, hand-engineered feature pipeline. CodeBERT's result is more nuanced: its Macro F1 (0.7780) sits close behind the top two models, its accuracy (0.7976) is the highest of the five, and, as Section 8.5 established, its CWE-89 recall specifically is the strongest of any model evaluated.

This pattern is consistent with the theoretical basis established in Chapter 2's literature review (Section 2.9): pretrained Transformer representations can reduce the amount of task-specific labelled data needed to reach strong performance compared with training entirely from scratch, and CodeBERT's competitive overall standing, despite this project's comparatively small, domain-specific fine-tuning dataset, is consistent with that literature. What the evidence does not support is a claim that pretraining provided a decisive, dataset-independent advantage sufficient to displace the classical hybrid pipeline: CodeBERT did not lead the aggregate Macro F1 comparison, and its CWE-295 recall (0.7344) was itself unremarkable relative to SVM and LSTM on that specific class (Section 8.6). The evidence therefore suggests that pretrained code representations can be competitive, and in some sub-tasks (CWE-89 recall specifically) genuinely superior, on a small, domain-specific Android vulnerability dataset, while not providing sufficient overall advantage in this evaluation to replace the classical hybrid XGBoost pipeline actually selected for production. This finding should not be generalised beyond this dataset, this feature/fine-tuning protocol, and this evaluation split; a larger Android-specific CWE corpus, additional pretraining epochs, or a different fine-tuning strategy could plausibly shift this balance, and this chapter makes no claim about what a differently-resourced evaluation would find.

## 8.8 Decision Engine Findings

The Hybrid Decision Engine is not a sixth independent machine-learning classifier competing with the five models discussed in Sections 8.3–8.7; it is a decision-support layer that combines XGBoost's own class probability with independently-computed static and data-flow evidence into a final `VULNERABLE`/`SAFE`/`SUSPICIOUS_REVIEW` decision, and its evaluation asks a structurally different question from the model comparison above.

**Figure 8.2. Pareto Frontier: Coverage vs. FNR (Validation Set).** *(figure_8_2_pareto_frontier.png)*

Figure 8.2 (`coverage_optimization/plots/pareto_frontier.png`) illustrates why the two operating points discussed below were selected the way they were: across 1,659 evaluated threshold pairs, the literal coverage/FNR Pareto frontier collapses to a single dominant point, because widening the auto-decision threshold in this dataset tends to improve coverage and FNR together up to a point, before false-positive rate degrades sharply. This is why the production and balanced candidate operating points reported in Table 8.5 were selected using FPR bounds anchored to real baselines rather than the raw frontier alone, a methodological detail already established in Chapter 7 and repeated here only to support this chapter's interpretation of the trade-off.

**Table 8.5. Decision Engine Findings.**
| Operating point | Coverage | FNR (auto-decided) | FPR (auto-decided) | Precision | Status |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Production (deployed) | 32.7% | 3.03% | 9.09% | ≈0.941 | **Deployed** |
| Research-balanced candidate | 88.7% | 20.24% | 13.85% | — | **Not deployed** |

The production operating point is **highly conservative**: it commits to an automatic decision for only 32.7% of cases, deferring the remainder (`SUSPICIOUS_REVIEW`) to manual review rather than forcing a verdict — but among the cases it does auto-decide, its error control is strong, with an FNR of 3.03%, substantially lower than any of the five raw classifiers' own FNR reported in Table 8.2. This is the intended behaviour: the engine trades coverage for reliability specifically on the false-negative axis, consistent with the security-relevant rationale that a missed vulnerability carries a materially worse consequence than an unresolved case deferred to a human reviewer. The research-balanced candidate demonstrates that substantially higher coverage (88.7%, nearly triple the production point) is achievable, at a materially higher FNR (20.24%, closer to the raw classifiers' own error rates) and a review workload reduced to 11.31% of cases. **This balanced configuration was not deployed.** Its robustness across alternative data splits was evaluated and classified **INCONCLUSIVE**, with an overall production candidacy rating of **"B. PROMISING BUT MORE DATA REQUIRED."**

This chapter interprets the undeployed status of the balanced candidate as itself a finding worth stating plainly, not as an unresolved loose end: the evidence indicates the balanced candidate is a genuinely promising direction — its FNR improvement over the raw classifiers and its dramatically higher coverage than the production point are both real, measured results — but the INCONCLUSIVE robustness classification means this project's evidence does not yet support deploying it with confidence that its measured advantage would hold on data the engine has not yet seen. Choosing to keep the conservative, calibrated production thresholds in place rather than adopting the higher-coverage candidate is, on this evidence, a defensible engineering decision rather than a missed improvement, and this chapter reports it as exactly that: a documented trade-off between coverage and false-negative control, resolved conservatively pending further evidence, not a settled optimum.

## 8.9 Real-Time Detection and System Integration Findings

The real-time detection pipeline's own internal integration — feature construction, XGBoost inference, SHAP explanation, and Decision Engine evaluation composing correctly through the shared `predict_code()` entry point — is established by Chapter 7's automated integration testing (`test_endpoint_consistency.py`, `test_xgboost_production_integration.py`) and is not re-examined here in isolation. What this chapter adds is an interpretation of what that integration achieves relative to the research aim stated in Chapter 1: a single shared detection pipeline, reachable by both the developer-facing plugin and (indirectly, through the backend's shared prediction logic) the same infrastructure the mobile application's `/apps/*` routes ultimately draw from, means the same model, the same feature representation, and the same Decision Engine thresholds govern every finding this system produces, rather than a developer-facing verdict and a user-facing verdict being computed by two independently-maintained detection paths that could silently diverge. This design choice, evaluated at the automated-test layer in Chapter 7 and demonstrated working end-to-end at the live-validation layer in Section 8.11, directly supports the research aim's requirement (Chapter 1, Section 1.3) for a single, integrated system rather than two loosely-coupled tools sharing a name.

## 8.10 IntelliJ Plugin Findings

The plugin's 55 automated Kotlin tests, including the 9-test `LiveAnnotatorIntegrationTest.kt` suite driving real plugin classes against the real backend inside a headless IntelliJ Platform instance (Chapter 7, Section 7.10), demonstrate that the plugin's core logic — annotation lifecycle, Quick Fix locator/action separation, and re-scan verification — behaves correctly under a genuine, if headless, platform runtime, which is a real escalation in rigour over a plain unit test. This chapter's interpretation is bounded exactly where Chapter 7's was: this evidence does not extend to a claim of visible-GUI validation. No human-observed session inside a visible, running IDE window, and no plugin-side screenshot, exists anywhere in this project's validation history. The practical implication is that this project can state with confidence that the plugin's underlying logic is correct against the real backend, while stating with equal honesty that the specific, human-perceptible experience of seeing an annotation, tooltip, or Quick Fix preview render inside a live IDE window remains unconfirmed. This is reported as a scope boundary of the validation performed, not as evidence that the plugin's UI-facing behaviour is defective — the two are different claims, and this chapter does not conflate them.

## 8.11 Mobile Application and Chatbot Findings

The mobile application and chatbot are the subject of this project's strongest live-validation evidence, and this chapter interprets that evidence carefully, distinguishing what was genuinely demonstrated from what was not attempted or was attempted and blocked.

Two distinct bodies of live evidence support this: Phase 14B established that the application's core, most-frequently-used flows — login, registration, session restore, logout, dashboard, known-app search, application details, security assessment, compatible-device compatibility, alternatives, cross-user history isolation, and the chatbot's basic mechanism — work correctly against the real backend and database on a real device. Phase 21 then established the full chatbot experience end-to-end, not merely its mechanism: the Home → Security Chat FAB, all six remaining required chatbot questions (Q3–Q8), both remaining suggestion chips (C3/C4), a twelve-turn multi-turn conversation, chat-history reload after navigation, unknown-question fallback, empty-input handling, a genuine network-failure-and-recovery cycle, long-response scrolling, and keyboard/multi-line input, with no overflow or crash observed across more than 30 minutes of continuous interaction and 48 genuine screenshots — including its behaviour under adverse conditions (an unknown question, an empty submission, and a deliberate backend outage).

The evaluation indicates that the assembled system genuinely operates as intended for an end user across a substantial majority of its implemented functionality, including behaviour a purely automated test cannot easily construct — a real network interruption observed and recovered from on a real device is qualitatively different evidence from a mocked `NetworkException` in a Dart-VM widget test, even though both were separately verified (Chapter 7, Sections 7.11–7.12). This chapter does not convert this result into a claim of universal usability, broad field deployment readiness, or validation across device models, Android versions, or real end users beyond the single test account and emulator configuration used in these sessions — no such broader evidence exists in this project, and none is claimed here.

Four specific live-validation cases remain outstanding: Search-B (unknown-application search), Search-D (network-error search), Compatibility-B (INCOMPATIBLE), and Compatibility-C (NOT_AVAILABLE). In the most recent attempt to close these gaps, the Android emulator entered a persistent System UI failure before the application could even be launched, with free physical RAM measured at approximately 4 GB and stable throughout — **this chapter does not attribute that failure to insufficient RAM**, since the measurement directly contradicts a resource-shortage explanation, and instead reports it as an unresolved environment-level instability affecting this specific validation attempt. This is treated as a threat to the completeness of this project's live-validation coverage, not as evidence that the application's search or compatibility functionality is itself defective: no application code was reached during the blocked attempt, so no defect in that code could have been, or was, observed. Phase 21's results are unaffected by this later, unrelated session's outcome and are not downgraded by it anywhere in this thesis.

## 8.14 Comparison with Related Literature

Chapter 2 identified five specific gaps in the reviewed literature (Section 2.15): an integration gap, a vulnerability-coverage gap, an explainability gap, a real-time developer-workflow gap, and an evaluation-consistency gap. This section compares DualVulnDroid's actual, verified results against each, using only the studies already discussed in Chapter 2 — no new citation is introduced here.

Against the **evaluation-consistency gap**, Chapter 2 noted that the reviewed ML/DL vulnerability-detection literature uses different datasets, label taxonomies, and metrics across studies (Devign's own accuracy/F1, VulBERTa's benchmark suite, Senanayake et al.'s aggregate F1), making cross-paper comparison difficult. This project's five-model comparison (Section 8.3), evaluated under one shared protocol and a single frozen test split, directly addresses this specific gap within its own scope — the evidence supports that a controlled, single-protocol comparison across classical ML, a from-scratch deep-learning model, and a pretrained Transformer is achievable and produces interpretable, comparable results, consistent with what Chapter 2 identified as a methodological requirement rather than an optional addition.

Against the **vulnerability-coverage gap**, Chapter 2 (Pattern 6, Section 2.14) found that CWE-89 and CWE-295 are consistently studied separately across the reviewed literature, including in Senanayake et al.'s broader multi-CWE work, where no isolated, per-category evaluation distinguishing the two was reported. This project's per-CWE evaluation (Sections 8.5–8.6) directly provides that isolated comparison, and the finding that no model performs best on both classes (Section 8.6) is itself a result this comparison-gap literature did not previously establish for either CWE-89 or CWE-295 specifically, since the reviewed studies did not report it.

Against the **integration gap**, Chapter 2 identified ACVED (Senanayake et al., 2023a) as the closest comparable prior system — a genuine, real-time, ML-based, Android-CWE-aware IDE plugin — but noted that its published description does not report combining ML prediction with a separately surfaced static/data-flow evidence trace, a model-level explanation mechanism, or automated Quick Fix remediation. DualVulnDroid's Decision Engine (Section 8.8), which explicitly combines ML probability with data-flow evidence into one scored decision, and its Quick Fix subsystem (Section 8.10), directly address the specific integration this literature identified as absent from the closest prior work. This chapter states this comparison carefully: ACVED's absence of these features is reported in Chapter 2 as "not reported in the available published description," not as a confirmed absence, and this project's comparison against it is accordingly framed as addressing a documented gap in what is publicly known about that system, not as a claim of demonstrated superiority over a system whose full internal design was not independently verifiable from the reviewed literature.

Against the **explainability gap**, Chapter 2 (Pattern 5) found that of twenty studies compared, only SHAP's own foundational work and IVDetect reported an explicit explainability mechanism, and ACVED's published description did not report one. DualVulnDroid's use of real SHAP against the production booster (Section 8.9; Chapter 7, Section 7.8) is consistent with the design implication Chapter 2 drew from this gap (Table 2.3: "provide SHAP-based model explanation, kept explicitly distinct from static/data-flow evidence"), and this project's implementation follows that separation directly rather than merging the two evidence sources into one undifferentiated output.

Against the **real-time developer-workflow gap**, Chapter 2 found that of the studies compared, real-time IDE integration was confirmed only for ACVED, with the majority of ML/DL studies (Devign, IVDetect, CodeBERT's own evaluation, GraphCodeBERT, VulBERTa) evaluating offline against held-out datasets without demonstrating continuous, in-editor feedback. This project's plugin (Section 8.10) provides the same category of real-time integration, though — consistent with this chapter's own standard of honesty — its validation to date is headless-platform-level rather than visible-GUI-level, a distinction Chapter 2's own comparison table does not need to resolve for prior work but which this chapter states plainly for its own system.

Chapter 2's literature review does not report a directly comparable published study evaluating a from-scratch LSTM and a fine-tuned CodeBERT together against classical ML on an Android-specific, dual-CWE dataset under one protocol; where the available literature does not support a direct numerical comparison, this chapter states that explicitly rather than constructing one. Chakraborty et al.'s (2022) general finding that deep-learning vulnerability detectors can underperform their benchmark results under more realistic conditions is the one point of direct, qualitative alignment between this project's LSTM result (Section 8.7) and the literature reviewed in Chapter 2, and this chapter treats that alignment as genuine corroboration of a documented, prior finding, not as evidence this project's result was somehow predetermined by it.

## 8.15 Research Contribution

Within the scope of this project, DualVulnDroid's evidence-supported contributions are: a dual-vulnerability detection focus treating CWE-89 and CWE-295 as a joint, per-category-reported evaluation target, addressing a gap Chapter 2 identified as consistently unaddressed in the reviewed literature (Section 8.14); a controlled, single-protocol, five-model comparative evaluation (Linear SVM, Random Forest, XGBoost, LSTM, CodeBERT) on a shared, leakage-checked dataset split; a hybrid feature representation combining learned lexical features with 23 deterministic security/data-flow features, verified in production at 7,610 dimensions; a production XGBoost integration with real, content-hash-versioned model artefacts and a documented, tested load-time correctness fix (the 103-of-123 boosting-round slice); real SHAP explainability computed against the exact production model rather than an approximation; a Hybrid Decision Engine combining ML prediction with independent evidence at a documented, calibrated, and honestly-reported operating point; a real-time IntelliJ/Android Studio plugin integration, validated at the headless-automated level; a Flutter mobile application providing application security assessment, compatibility checking, alternatives, and a grounded, non-fabricating chatbot; a chat-history read/write capability validated live end-to-end; and an end-to-end architecture whose major flows were demonstrated working together on a real Android device, not only in isolated component tests.

This chapter does not claim DualVulnDroid is the first or only system to combine these elements; Chapter 2 identified ACVED as a genuine, comparable prior system on several of these dimensions, and this project's contribution is stated relative to the specific gaps Chapter 2's comparative analysis established in what is documented about that and other reviewed systems, not as an unqualified claim of novelty. Where this project's evidence extends beyond what the reviewed literature documents — the joint CWE-89/CWE-295 evaluation, the explicit static/data-flow-plus-ML Decision Engine, and the model-level SHAP explanation kept separate from evidence-level explanation — that extension is stated as being relative to the documented literature specifically, consistent with the cautious framing Chapter 2 itself adopted throughout.

## 8.16 Practical and Software Engineering Implications

For **Android developers**, the evidence in Sections 8.9–8.10 indicates that real-time, in-editor detection combining a classifier's prediction with independently-verifiable data-flow evidence is achievable without requiring a separate, manually-triggered scanning step, and that a narrowly-scoped, re-verified Quick Fix (confirmed by an actual re-scan rather than assumed to work) can reduce both the effort and the risk of manual remediation. For **secure software development practice** more broadly, the Decision Engine's explicit `SUSPICIOUS_REVIEW` state (Section 8.8) demonstrates a concrete pattern for handling the coverage/reliability tension inherent to any automated security decision system: rather than forcing every case into a binary verdict, an honestly-reported "defer to human review" outcome is itself a legitimate, measurable design choice, not an admission of failure.

For **IDE-based vulnerability detection tooling**, the plugin's Locator/Quick-Fix class separation (deciding whether a fix is safe to apply, kept structurally separate from the mechanical rewrite itself) offers a reusable implementation pattern for tools attempting automated remediation without over-claiming safety. For **security tooling design generally**, this project's consistent "report honestly rather than fabricate" pattern — an unassessed application returns "not assessed" rather than a guessed risk level, a missing device profile returns `NOT_AVAILABLE` rather than an assumed compatibility verdict — is a design principle with implications beyond this specific system, directly evidenced by its consistent application across the backend, plugin, and mobile application (Chapters 5–7) and confirmed operating correctly under live, unscripted interaction in Phase 21 (Section 8.11).

For **explainable ML in security contexts**, this project's separation of model-level explanation (SHAP) from evidence-level explanation (data-flow tracing) is a direct, implemented instance of the design implication Chapter 2 drew from the explainability literature (Table 2.3), and its correct operation is verified at both the automated and live-validation layers. For **real-time developer feedback and hybrid ML/static evidence approaches**, this project's central architectural finding — that combining a classifier prediction with independent corroborating evidence measurably reduces the effective miss rate relative to the raw classifier alone (Section 8.8) — has implications for any security tool considering whether to deploy a raw model score directly or to layer a decision process on top of it. For **mobile security assessment**, the evidence in Section 8.11 that a real, functioning chatbot grounded strictly in stored, verified data (never fabricating an app-specific answer it cannot support) operates correctly under real, unscripted questions has implications for the design of user-facing security-information tools more broadly, where the temptation to generate a plausible-sounding but unverified answer is a genuine risk this project's design and validation evidence both explicitly guard against.

## 8.17 Limitations and Threats to Validity

This chapter's conclusions are bounded by several limitations, each already introduced across earlier sections and consolidated here for clarity rather than newly disclosed.

**Table 8.8. Research Limitations and Implications.**
| Limitation | Implication |
|---|---|
| Frozen test set of 168 samples | All five-model and per-CWE metrics (Sections 8.3–8.6) are precise on this specific split but carry inherent sampling variance on a set of this size; individual percentage-point differences between closely-ranked models (e.g. XGBoost vs SVM Macro F1) should be read as directionally informative rather than statistically certain, since no significance test is reported in the underlying research artifacts |
| Dataset scale and composition | LSTM and CodeBERT's relative standing (Section 8.7) reflects performance on this project's specific dataset scale and composition; a materially larger or differently-sourced corpus could plausibly shift these results |
| CodeBERT provenance | An earlier, disputed CodeBERT result existed among this project's working artifacts; this thesis uses only the corrected, independently-verified figures (accuracy 0.7976, CWE-89 recall 0.8621) throughout, and the correction is stated explicitly here rather than left implicit |
| Decision Engine robustness | The higher-coverage balanced candidate's cross-split robustness was classified INCONCLUSIVE ("B. PROMISING BUT MORE DATA REQUIRED"); it remains undeployed, and this chapter does not claim it is a settled improvement |
| Production operating point coverage | The deployed Decision Engine auto-decides only 32.7% of cases; the remaining 67.3% depend on manual review, which this project's evidence base does not itself evaluate for reviewer accuracy or workload sustainability |
| Live mobile validation completeness | Search-B, Search-D, Compatibility-B, and Compatibility-C remain not validated live, due to a specific, measured emulator instability unrelated to RAM availability, not to an observed application defect |
| Plugin visible-GUI validation | No human-observed, visible-IDE-window session exists for the plugin; validation is headless-automated only |
| No user study or survey | This project does not include, and this thesis does not report, any participant study, questionnaire, or user-feedback data; all developer- and user-facing claims in this chapter are grounded in automated tests, research-evaluation artifacts, and the researcher's own live-validation sessions, not in third-party human evaluation |
| Generalisation | Performance figures (Chapter 7, Section 7.15) and model comparisons were measured on this project's own development host and dataset; no claim is made that these figures would reproduce identically on different hardware, a different Android code corpus, or a production-scale deployment |

None of these limitations is treated in this chapter as evidence of a software defect; where an application-level defect actually occurred during this project's history (the Phase 14B chatbot suggestion-chip wording mismatch), it was found, fixed, and regression-tested, and is reported as such in Chapter 7 rather than listed here as an unresolved limitation.

## 8.18 Chapter Summary

This chapter has interpreted, rather than repeated, the testing and evaluation evidence established in Chapter 7. The five-model comparison demonstrates that no single model dominates every metric, and that XGBoost's production selection is a defensible, multi-factor decision rather than a claim of universal superiority — a distinction this chapter maintained explicitly throughout, including its direct acknowledgement that SVM achieved a materially lower FNR. The per-CWE comparison shows the production model's strengths and weaknesses differ meaningfully between CWE-89 and CWE-295, and that CodeBERT, though not the aggregate Macro F1 leader, achieved the strongest CWE-89 recall of any model evaluated, using the corrected, provenance-verified figure throughout. The Decision Engine findings establish a deliberately conservative, currently-deployed production operating point against an undeployed, higher-coverage candidate awaiting further robustness evidence. The live-validation findings distinguish Phase 21's genuinely successful, screenshot-evidenced session from a later, unrelated session's environment-level failure, preserving every genuine PASS result while reporting four specific, named gaps honestly rather than reframing them as covered. Against Chapter 1's ten research objectives and seven research questions, and against the five gaps Chapter 2 identified in the reviewed literature, this chapter has shown where this project's evidence provides a direct, supported answer and where it does not, without overstating either. Chapter 9 can proceed to consolidate these findings alongside this project's broader limitations and directions for future work, building on the interpretation established here without needing to re-derive it.
