CHAPTER 03 – METHODOLOGY

## 3.1 Introduction

This chapter describes how the research reported in this thesis was actually carried out. It combines several methodological traditions that are each individually well established but are less commonly reported together within a single, coherent account: empirical software engineering, in the sense of a staged, evidence-driven investigation of a defined problem [3]; dataset-based machine-learning experimentation, covering data preparation, model training, and controlled comparison; static and data-flow security analysis, providing deterministic, independently checkable evidence; explainable artificial intelligence, providing model-level insight into individual predictions; software system implementation, covering the backend, developer plugin, and end-user application; automated testing at multiple levels; and, where the environment genuinely permitted it, validation in real execution environments rather than simulated ones alone. The methodology was deliberately designed around five recurring concerns that resurface throughout this chapter: reproducibility, so that the reported process can be re-run and checked; leakage prevention, so that no evaluation result is inflated by information that would not genuinely be available at prediction time; fair model comparison, so that every evaluated model is judged under an identical protocol; security-aware evaluation, so that the specific consequences of a missed vulnerability are treated with appropriate weight rather than folded into a single aggregate accuracy figure; and evidence-based conclusions, so that every claim made later in this thesis can be traced back to a specific, described step in this chapter. This chapter describes how the work was conducted; it does not report the resulting performance figures, which are presented and discussed in full in Chapter 6 (ML/DL Model Development and Evaluation), Chapter 7 (Hybrid Decision Engine and Explainability), Chapter 9 (Testing and Validation), and Chapter 10 (Results and Discussion). Where a specific number is stated below, such as a dataset size or a split configuration, it is stated only because it is itself a methodological parameter of the study design, not a result.

## 3.2 Research Design

The overall research design is that of an empirical and experimental software-engineering study, structured as a sequence of staged phases in which each phase's output is a defined, verifiable artefact consumed by the phase that follows it. This staged structure was chosen deliberately, following general guidance on structuring empirical software-engineering studies as a scoped, planned, and systematically executed sequence of steps rather than an unstructured, exploratory process [3], because the research combines several substantially different kinds of work, data preparation, statistical model training, security-specific static analysis, and multi-component software implementation, each of which benefits from being completed, and where appropriate frozen, before the next phase begins.

The major phases followed in this research were: (1) problem investigation, establishing the specific detection requirements for CWE-89 and CWE-295; (2) dataset collection and preparation, including cleaning and conflict resolution; (3) security feature engineering, building the deterministic feature set used alongside text-based features; (4) machine-learning model development, training the three classical candidate models; (5) research model comparison, evaluating all candidate models, including the supplementary deep-learning models, under a shared protocol; (6) security-aware Decision Engine analysis, evaluating how model predictions and deterministic evidence could be combined; (7) robustness and sensitivity analysis, examining how stable a candidate set of thresholds was across alternative data splits; (8) production XGBoost selection and integration, incorporating the selected model into the live backend without retraining it; (9) backend development, implementing the FastAPI service and MySQL persistence layer; (10) IntelliJ plugin development, implementing real-time and project-wide developer-facing analysis; (11) Flutter application development, implementing the end-user-facing application; (12) chatbot development, implementing the security-focused conversational feature grounded in real backend data; (13) automated validation, exercising the backend, plugin, and application through their respective automated test suites; (14) real-environment validation, exercising the system in a genuine IntelliJ Platform test fixture and, separately, on a real Android emulator, where the execution environment available at the time of the work made this possible; and (15) final documentation, consolidating the research and implementation record into this thesis. A staged design of this kind was appropriate here specifically because several later phases depend on an earlier phase's output being fixed and unchanging, most importantly the frozen dataset split described in Section 3.9, without which fair comparison across models trained at different times would not be possible.

## 3.3 Research Approach and Methodology

This research combines a quantitative evaluation approach with a qualitative, engineering-oriented evaluation approach, applied to different aspects of the work.

The quantitative approach is used to evaluate the trained classification models and the Hybrid Decision Engine, using standard classification metrics, accuracy, precision, recall, F1 score (both macro-averaged across the four classes and computed for the binary vulnerable/safe view), and Receiver Operating Characteristic Area Under the Curve (ROC-AUC), together with two metrics given particular attention in this research specifically because of its security context: the False Positive Rate (FPR) and the False Negative Rate (FNR). For the Hybrid Decision Engine specifically, two further quantitative measures are used: coverage, the proportion of cases the engine is willing to decide automatically, and review workload, the complementary proportion of cases deferred for manual review.

The qualitative and engineering evaluation approach is used where a numeric metric alone would not adequately capture whether a component of the system behaves as intended. This includes assessing the quality and coherence of the explanations produced, both the SHAP-based model explanation and the deterministic static/data-flow evidence; the usability of the developer-facing plugin output; the correctness and safety of error handling across the backend, plugin, and application; the user interface behaviour observed during manual and automated testing; and the correctness of the compatibility interpretation produced for a given application and device combination, including its explicit honesty when required data is not available.

A security-focused evaluation gives particular attention to false negatives throughout this research, in both the quantitative and qualitative components described above. A missed vulnerability, a genuinely vulnerable sample the system reports as safe, represents a more serious practical failure than a false alarm, a genuinely safe sample flagged as vulnerable, because a missed vulnerability can allow a real weakness to reach a production application undetected, whereas a false alarm merely costs a developer or reviewer additional, avoidable attention. This asymmetry is reflected throughout the methodology: FNR is reported alongside accuracy and F1 rather than omitted, and the Hybrid Decision Engine's own automatic-decision reliability is evaluated with particular attention to its FNR on the cases it decides automatically, rather than only its overall coverage.

**Table 3.4. Evaluation metrics used in this research.**

| Metric | Definition | Applied To |
|---|---|---|
| Accuracy | Proportion of all predictions that are correct | Classical and deep-learning models (four-class) |
| Precision / Recall / F1 (macro) | Precision, recall, and their harmonic mean, averaged equally across all four classes | Classical and deep-learning models (four-class) |
| Binary F1 | F1 computed on the derived VULNERABLE/SAFE view (Section 3.8) | All evaluated models |
| ROC-AUC | Area under the Receiver Operating Characteristic curve for the vulnerable-class score | All evaluated models |
| False Positive Rate (FPR) | Proportion of genuinely safe samples incorrectly flagged as vulnerable | All evaluated models; Decision Engine auto-decided cases |
| False Negative Rate (FNR) | Proportion of genuinely vulnerable samples incorrectly flagged as safe — given particular weight throughout this research | All evaluated models; Decision Engine auto-decided cases |
| Coverage | Proportion of cases the Hybrid Decision Engine decides automatically, without deferring to manual review | Hybrid Decision Engine only |
| Review workload | Proportion of cases the Hybrid Decision Engine defers to manual review (1 − coverage) | Hybrid Decision Engine only |

## 3.4 System Development Methodology

The DualVulnDroid system was developed using an iterative and incremental software development approach, in which working, individually testable increments of functionality were implemented, integrated, and verified in sequence, rather than through a single, upfront, monolithic build-then-test cycle. This description is intentionally specific: no claim is made here that a named, formally defined methodology such as Scrum was followed, since no fixed-length sprint cadence, dedicated backlog grooming process, or formal role structure of that kind was actually used in this project; the iterative and incremental characterisation reflects what was actually practised, which is a general engineering pattern broader than any one named process framework.

Implementation proceeded through a sequence of incremental phases, each adding a coherent unit of functionality on top of the previous ones and each followed by testing before the next phase began: the FastAPI backend and MySQL database layer; APK manifest metadata extraction; the compatibility-analysis module; the alternative-application-ranking module; the Flutter frontend application; the security-focused chatbot; the classical and deep-learning model research and evaluation work described in Sections 3.11 and 3.12; the production integration of the selected XGBoost model into the existing backend; and the final rounds of automated and, where available, real-environment validation. Continuous testing and regression verification were used throughout this sequence: whenever a later increment was added, the automated test suites covering earlier increments, described in full in Section 3.19 and Chapter 9, were re-run to confirm that the new work had not silently altered previously verified behaviour, an approach consistent with standard regression-testing practice in iterative software development.

## 3.6 Data Collection

Three distinct kinds of data were used in this research, and they are kept clearly distinct throughout this thesis because each serves a different methodological purpose. The first, and the only one used for model training and evaluation, is the project's labelled vulnerability dataset, a collection of Android-relevant code samples together with a four-class label (defined in Section 3.8) and, for many rows, an accompanying explanation of the labelled vulnerability or safe pattern. This dataset contains examples of both target vulnerability categories, SQL Injection (CWE-89) and Improper Certificate Validation (CWE-295), in both their vulnerable and safe forms, and is the sole source of the raw row count, class distribution, and split sizes reported in Sections 3.7 through 3.9.

The second kind of data is the set of manually constructed test fixtures used throughout the automated test suites described in Section 3.19 and Chapter 9, for example the hand-written vulnerable and safe SQL and certificate-validation code snippets used in the backend's own `pytest` suite, and the hand-written Kotlin/Java fixtures used in the IntelliJ plugin's Gradle test suite. These fixtures exist to exercise specific, known code paths and specific, known-shape inputs under test, and were never included in, and are structurally unrelated to, the labelled dataset used for model training; a fixture used to test that a particular Quick Fix pattern is correctly recognised, for example, is not a training example and was never treated as one.

The third kind of data is real APK verification material: genuine Android application packages used to exercise the manifest-extraction and compatibility-analysis pipeline described in Sections 3.16 and 3.19, together with the real, persisted database rows this exercising produced during backend and end-to-end validation. This material demonstrates that the manifest-extraction pipeline operates correctly against real, compiled application packages, rather than only against synthetic fixtures, and is kept clearly distinct from both the labelled training dataset and the hand-constructed unit-test fixtures described above.

## 3.7 Dataset Preparation and Cleaning

The raw labelled dataset used in this research contains 1,687 rows. Before this dataset could be used for a grouped, leakage-checked split, it was necessary to identify and address label noise present in the raw data. Label noise here specifically refers to rows whose code text, once normalised, is identical or near-identical to another row's normalised code text, but which carries a different label; since the same underlying code template cannot genuinely be both vulnerable and safe, such a conflict indicates an annotation inconsistency in the source data rather than a genuine, informative distinction for a model to learn from.

Conflict detection was performed using the project's `normalize_code()` function, the same text-normalisation function used later in the TF-IDF feature pipeline (Section 3.10), applied here as a grouping key: every row is normalised, and rows that share an identical or near-identical normalised template are treated as belonging to the same underlying code-template group. Both an exact-duplicate check, comparing normalised text directly, and a near-duplicate-template check were applied. Any group in which more than one distinct label appeared among its member rows was flagged as a conflicting-label group, and every row belonging to such a group, 559 rows across the full raw dataset, was excluded from the dataset used for splitting, training, and evaluation. This left a clean dataset of 1,128 rows, used for every subsequent step described in this chapter.

Consistent with the emphasis on reproducibility set out in Section 3.1, these exclusions were not silently discarded: the excluded rows were preserved in a separate, dedicated file rather than deleted, so that the exact set of rows removed, and the reason each was removed, remains inspectable and reproducible rather than being an untraceable side effect of the cleaning process. ## 3.8 Data Labelling and Classification

Every row in the clean dataset carries one of four labels, reflecting a combination of the targeted vulnerability category, defined against MITRE's authoritative CWE-89 and CWE-295 definitions [1], [2], and its vulnerable or safe state:

**Table 3.2. Class definitions.**

| Label | Meaning |
|---|---|
| s0 | SQL Injection (CWE-89) — vulnerable |
| v0 | Improper Certificate Validation (CWE-295) — vulnerable |
| s1 | SQL Injection (CWE-89) — safe |
| v1 | Improper Certificate Validation (CWE-295) — safe |

This four-class formulation was retained as the primary classification target throughout model training and evaluation, since it is the only label representation that distinguishes both the vulnerability category and the vulnerable/safe state simultaneously; collapsing it prematurely would discard information a downstream consumer of a finding, whether a developer or the Hybrid Decision Engine, genuinely needs.

A second, derived binary view is used specifically for security-focused evaluation metrics such as FPR, FNR, and binary F1: VULNERABLE = {s0, v0} and SAFE = {s1, v1}. Both views are needed because they answer different, complementary questions: the four-class, multiclass view identifies which specific vulnerability category and state a given sample represents, information the rest of the system depends on to produce a specific CWE label and a specific explanation, whereas the binary view evaluates the narrower, security-critical question of whether the system detects the presence of a vulnerability at all, regardless of which of the two targeted categories it belongs to, which is the view most directly comparable to the FPR/FNR trade-off discussed in Section 3.3.

## 3.9 Train / Validation / Test Split

The clean dataset (1,128 rows) was split into training, validation, and test subsets of sizes 791, 169, and 168 rows respectively, using a fixed random seed of 42 throughout, so that the exact split produced is itself reproducible.

The split was constructed to be group-aware rather than a naive random row-level split, using the same `normalize_code()`-derived template grouping described in Section 3.7 as the grouping key for a group-based shuffle split (the project's `GroupShuffleSplit`-style grouped splitting, implemented in `ml.dataset_prep`/`research_eval.data_split`), so that every row belonging to the same normalised code-template group is assigned to exactly one of the three subsets, never split across more than one. Stratification and group-aware balancing were applied as actually implemented in the project's own split-construction code, so that the resulting class distribution across the three subsets remains reasonably representative of the overall clean dataset while still respecting the group constraint. Two leakage checks were then performed on the resulting split and are reported as part of the split's own metadata: a check for any group-key overlap across the three subsets, and a check for any exact-text overlap across the three subsets, with both checks confirming zero overlap on the split actually used throughout this research.

The purpose of this grouped, leakage-checked construction is to prevent a specific, concrete form of evaluation leakage: without it, a near-duplicate of a training-set code template could appear in the validation or test subset, allowing a model to appear to generalise by effectively recognising a memorised template rather than a genuinely new example, which would inflate every reported evaluation metric relative to the model's real ability to generalise to unseen code. Every row in the clean dataset is assigned to exactly one of the three subsets; none are discarded at this stage.

A limitation of this construction, stated plainly here because it materially affects how the split should be interpreted, is that no true application-level split was possible: the dataset does not contain a reliable application or project identifier for each row, so grouping by normalised code template is the closest leakage-preventing grouping the available data supports, rather than a grouping that guarantees no two rows in different subsets originate from the same source application.

## 3.10 Feature Engineering

The production feature pipeline used for the classical machine-learning models combines two families of features into a single feature vector: text-based features derived from three separately fitted vectorizers, and a fixed set of 23 deterministic security- and data-flow-derived features. The text-based portion comprises a word-level TF-IDF vectorizer (up to 5,000 features, word n-grams of length one to three), a character-level TF-IDF vectorizer (up to 2,000 features, character n-grams of length two to five), and a count vectorizer (up to 1,000 features, word n-grams of length one to two), each applied to the code text after the same `normalize_code()` normalisation described in Section 3.7, which lowercases the text, strips comments, and replaces string, character, and numeric literals with fixed placeholder tokens so that superficial literal values do not themselves become spurious learned features. All three vectorizers, together with the deterministic security-feature extractor described below, combine to a total production feature dimensionality of 7,610.

Critically for the leakage-prevention goal set out in Section 3.1, all three vectorizers are fitted on the training split only and are then reused, unmodified, to transform the validation split, the test split, and any later inference input; at no point is a vectorizer re-fitted on validation or test data, which would otherwise leak vocabulary information from held-out data into the feature representation.

The 23 deterministic security features are computed directly from the code text by a dedicated feature-extraction module and are organised around the two targeted vulnerability categories together with a set of data-flow-derived structural indicators. For SQL Injection, the feature set includes indicators for a detected untrusted source, a detected SQL sink, a traced source-to-sink relationship, string concatenation used to build a query, use of a parameterised query pattern, presence of user-input-derived data, use of a raw-query API, use of an `execSQL`-style API, and use of a compiled-statement API. For Improper Certificate Validation, the feature set includes indicators for a `HostnameVerifier` construct, an unconditional `return true` pattern, a `TrustManager` construct, an empty trust-check body, an exception-based validation pattern, and use of the platform's default verifier, together with three further indicators covering WebView SSL error handling: a detected SSL error callback, a handler that proceeds despite the error, and a handler that cancels in response to the error. A final group of five features is derived from the project's own data-flow analysis rather than from raw keyword presence: overall data-flow depth, presence of an interprocedural flow, and counts of flows classified as unsafe, safe, and suspicious respectively. Every feature listed here corresponds to a feature actually implemented in the project's feature-extraction module; no feature is described here that the implementation does not compute.

## 3.11 Machine Learning Model Development

Three classical machine-learning models were developed and evaluated as candidates for production integration: a linear Support Vector Machine, a Random Forest, and XGBoost. All three were trained and evaluated on the identical frozen split and identical feature representation described in Sections 3.9 and 3.10, so that any difference in their evaluation results in Chapter 6 can be attributed to the model itself rather than to a difference in the underlying data or features. Each model's hyperparameters were selected without ever consulting the frozen test set, consistent with the leakage-prevention goal stated in Section 3.1.

The Linear SVM was selected as a strong, well-established linear baseline for high-dimensional, sparse text-style feature representations such as the TF-IDF vectors used here [6]. It was implemented as a `LinearSVC` with class weights set to balance the four classes, wrapped in a `CalibratedClassifierCV` (three-fold calibration, fitted on the training split only) so that a genuine class-probability estimate, needed for the vulnerable-class score used in ROC-AUC computation, could be obtained from a model whose native decision function is not itself probabilistic. Its regularisation hyperparameter, C, was selected by grid search over four candidate values using five-fold stratified cross-validation restricted to the training split, scored by macro-averaged F1.

The Random Forest was selected as a strong, non-linear ensemble baseline, well suited to a mixed feature representation of sparse text-derived features alongside the dense deterministic security features described in Section 3.10 [7]. It was implemented as a `RandomForestClassifier` with balanced class weights, with its number of estimators, maximum tree depth, and minimum samples per leaf selected by grid search under the same train-only, five-fold stratified cross-validation protocol used for the SVM, again scored by macro-averaged F1.

XGBoost was selected as the third classical candidate for its established strong empirical performance on structured and mixed-representation tabular data, and, as discussed further in Chapter 7, for its established support for SHAP-based explainability [5]. It was trained as a multi-class softprob objective covering all four classes, using validation-based early stopping, evaluated by multi-class log-loss on the validation split, rather than the train-only cross-validation protocol used for the other two models; this reflects XGBoost's own native early-stopping mechanism, which is the standard, established way of selecting the number of boosting rounds for a gradient-boosted tree model of this kind, and is consistent with how this candidate-selection approach was already used elsewhere in this project's production model-selection code prior to this research. A small grid of remaining hyperparameters, maximum tree depth and learning rate, was evaluated by the same validation-based macro-F1 criterion used for the other two models, again without ever consulting the test split during selection.

Class imbalance was addressed consistently across all three models by weighting each class inversely to its frequency in the training split, so that the comparatively less frequent classes were not systematically under-learned relative to the more frequent ones. In every case, the test split was used only once per model, after all hyperparameter selection had already been completed using the training and validation splits alone, to produce the final, reported evaluation figures presented in Chapter 6.

| Model | Implementation | Hyperparameter Search | Selection Criterion | Class Imbalance Handling |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Linear SVM | `LinearSVC` wrapped in `CalibratedClassifierCV` (3-fold, train-only) | Grid search over C ∈ {0.01, 0.1, 1.0, 10.0} | 5-fold stratified CV macro-F1, train split only | Balanced class weights |
| Random Forest | `RandomForestClassifier` | Grid search over n_estimators ∈ {200, 400}, max_depth ∈ {None, 20}, min_samples_leaf ∈ {1, 2} | 5-fold stratified CV macro-F1, train split only | Balanced class weights |
| XGBoost | Multi-class `multi:softprob` objective, native early stopping | Candidates over max_depth ∈ {4, 6, 8}, eta ∈ {0.05, 0.1}; up to 300 boosting rounds, early stopping after 20 rounds without improvement | Validation-set macro-F1 (validation-based early stopping, not train-only CV) | Per-class sample weights inversely proportional to class frequency |

## 3.12 Deep Learning Evaluation

Two deep-learning approaches were evaluated as supplementary research experiments on the identical frozen split described in Section 3.9, alongside, but methodologically separate from, the three classical models described in Section 3.11.

The first, a Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) network, was trained entirely from scratch using a custom, code-aware tokenizer written specifically for this project, deliberately distinct from the `normalize_code()` text normalisation used for the classical models' TF-IDF features: the tokenizer is case-sensitive, so that API identifiers such as `HostnameVerifier` or `X509TrustManager` remain distinguishable from unrelated lowercase tokens, while string, character, and numeric literals are still normalised to placeholder tokens to reduce sparse, sample-specific noise. The resulting token vocabulary was built from the training split's tokens only, capped at a fixed maximum vocabulary size, with any token encountered later that is not in this training-derived vocabulary mapped to a dedicated out-of-vocabulary token rather than being added to the vocabulary after the fact. The model architecture combines a trainable embedding layer, a bidirectional LSTM layer, dropout regularisation, and a dense classification head over the four target classes, trained with class weighting to address the same imbalance concern described in Section 3.11, using validation-based early stopping to select the final checkpoint. Training was performed on CPU only, using an already-installed Keras 3 environment with a TensorFlow backend, since GPU-accelerated training was not available in the development environment used for this research.

The second, CodeBERT, is a pretrained Transformer-based model for source code, obtained as the `microsoft/codebert-base` checkpoint via the Hugging Face `transformers` library and fine-tuned, using PyTorch, on the same frozen training split [8]. Unlike the from-scratch LSTM, CodeBERT's own tokenizer was used exactly as shipped with the pretrained checkpoint, without being re-fitted on this project's data at all; the fine-tuning process itself was still restricted strictly to the training split for weight updates and to the validation split for hyperparameter and checkpoint selection, with the maximum input sequence length itself chosen from a percentile of training-split tokenized lengths, capped at the checkpoint's own maximum supported length, so that no decision of any kind was informed by the test split before the model was frozen. Fine-tuning used a fixed learning rate, batch size, weight-decay, and warm-up schedule, with early stopping based on validation macro-F1, and the same train/validation/test evaluation protocol described for the classical models in Section 3.11 was applied identically once the model was frozen.

It is stated here explicitly, and is restated wherever relevant elsewhere in this thesis, that both the LSTM and CodeBERT were evaluated strictly as research-comparison models. Neither was integrated into the production detection pipeline described in later chapters; the production system, described in Chapter 4 onward, uses XGBoost exclusively, and the deep-learning results presented in Chapter 6 are reported as an empirical comparison against the classical models, not as a description of production technology.

## 3.13 Static and Data-Flow Analysis

Alongside the machine-learning models described above, DualVulnDroid applies deterministic static and data-flow analysis to the same code being classified, producing evidence that is used, as described in Section 3.15, to complement rather than to be blindly overridden by the machine-learning prediction. This analysis operates at two levels. The first is lexical and pattern-based analysis, checking for the presence of specific, well-defined security-relevant keywords and API usages, corresponding directly to the deterministic security features described in Section 3.10, such as a raw-query API call, a `HostnameVerifier` construct, or a WebView SSL-error handler. The second is source-and-sink, data-flow-oriented analysis, in which a source, a point where potentially untrusted data is introduced, such as a value derived from an Intent extra, is traced toward a sink, a point where that data is used in a security-sensitive operation, such as a SQL execution call, with the analysis recording whether an intervening safe transformation, such as parameterisation, appears along that path.

This static and data-flow evidence is used in this research to complement the machine-learning prediction, not to be either blindly trusted in isolation or blindly overridden by it: as described further in Section 3.15, both sources of evidence are combined by the Hybrid Decision Engine, and both are surfaced to the developer or the end user as separate, distinctly labelled pieces of evidence rather than being silently merged into an unexplained single verdict. It is stated explicitly here that this analysis does not constitute formal program verification: it does not construct or check a mathematically complete model of every possible program execution, and, consistent with the discussion of static and data-flow analysis limitations in Chapter 2, it can miss a genuine vulnerability whose relevant flow is not captured by the analysis's model of the code, or flag a pattern that a fuller, whole-program analysis would show to be safe.

## 3.14 Explainability Methodology

Two methodologically distinct forms of explanation are produced for a given finding in this research, and they are kept conceptually and terminologically separate throughout this thesis rather than merged into one another.

The first is SHAP-based model explanation, computed using a `TreeExplainer` applied to the production XGBoost model, which produces a signed contribution value for each input feature toward a specific prediction, following the unified additive feature-attribution framework introduced by Lundberg and Lee [9]. This is a model-level explanation: it describes which features of the input the trained model itself weighted most heavily in producing a specific prediction, grounded in the model's own learned parameters, and is reported and labelled in this research specifically as SHAP output, never as an independently verified fact about the code being analysed.

The second is security and data-flow evidence, comprising the deterministic source, sink, static-finding, vulnerability-type, and severity information produced by the analysis described in Section 3.13. This evidence is independently verifiable in the specific sense that it is not derived from a trained model's internal parameters at all, but from an explicit, inspectable rule or traced data-flow path, and is reported and labelled in this research as static or data-flow evidence, distinctly from the SHAP output described above. This distinction, between what a model's own explanation says about its own prediction and what an independent, deterministic analysis says about the code itself, is treated in this research as methodologically important and is never conflated: a finding's SHAP explanation and its data-flow evidence are always presented as two separate outputs, not combined into a single, undifferentiated explanation field.

## 3.15 Hybrid Decision Engine Methodology

The Hybrid Decision Engine combines the machine-learning model's prediction and confidence with the deterministic evidence described in Section 3.13 into a single evidence score, rather than treating the machine-learning prediction as the sole basis for a decision. Concretely, the engine accumulates a signed evidence score from several weighted sources: the machine-learning prediction, signed according to the predicted class and scaled by the predicted class's own probability; a traced SQL source-to-sink finding; an unparameterised-concatenation finding; confirmation that all SQL sinks in the sample are parameterised; a certificate-validation finding classified as vulnerable, suspicious, or safe respectively; and corroboration from the static keyword-based rule layer described in Section 3.13. The relative weights assigned to these sources reflect a deliberate design choice, kept unchanged throughout this research and not altered as part of it, that a genuinely traced, structural data-flow finding should carry more influence over the final decision than a merely average-confidence machine-learning prediction on its own.

The engine's total evidence score is compared against two thresholds to produce one of three conceptual decision states: **VULNERABLE**, when the score meets or exceeds the upper threshold; **SAFE**, when the score falls at or below the lower threshold; and **SUSPICIOUS_REVIEW**, for any score between the two thresholds, representing cases the engine explicitly declines to decide automatically and instead defers. The specific pair of thresholds actually loaded and used in the production system was calibrated from the validation split alone, never the test split, and is loaded from a dedicated, versioned calibration artefact at runtime; a fixed pair of reference default thresholds, documented directly in the Decision Engine's own source code, is used only as a fallback in the event that this calibration artefact is unavailable. It is stated explicitly here, and is not contradicted anywhere else in this thesis, that a separate, research-only "Balanced" set of thresholds was also evaluated as part of the robustness analysis described in Section 3.21, but was not deployed to the production system at any point during this research; the thresholds actually governing the deployed Decision Engine throughout this work are the calibrated production thresholds described above.

Security-aware evaluation of the Decision Engine, presented in full in Chapter 7, follows directly from the three-state design above: the engine's auto-decision coverage, the proportion of test-set cases it decides automatically rather than deferring, is reported alongside the reliability of those automatically decided cases specifically, including their FNR and FPR restricted to the automatically decided subset, and alongside the resulting review workload, the proportion of cases deferred to manual review. Reporting coverage and automatic-decision reliability together, rather than either in isolation, reflects the same reasoning set out in Section 3.11's broader discussion of the coverage/reliability trade-off: a high-coverage configuration that is not also evaluated for its reliability on the cases it decides would be a misleading basis for a practical security decision.

## 3.16 Android APK Metadata and Compatibility Analysis

Manifest-based metadata is extracted from a real, compiled Android application package using `pyaxmlparser`, a binary-AXML decoder capable of reading an APK's actual, compiled `AndroidManifest.xml` directly, rather than requiring access to the application's original source. The fields extracted through this pipeline are the package name, version name, version code, application label, declared permissions, minimum SDK version, target SDK version, the `debuggable` and `allowBackup` manifest attributes, the application's declared `<uses-feature>` entries, and, separately, the set of native-library ABIs present in the APK's own ZIP archive structure. Each of these is a raw, directly observed manifest fact; where a given field cannot be determined from the manifest actually present in a given APK, for example because the relevant attribute is simply absent, the corresponding field is recorded as genuinely unavailable rather than inferred or guessed.

This extraction methodology, and the certificate-related feature indicators described in Section 3.10, are informed directly by Android's own documented network-security defaults and configuration mechanism [10] and by the OWASP Mobile Application Security project's classification of insecure-communication risks [11]. A clear methodological distinction is maintained between these raw, extracted manifest facts and the derived compatibility decisions computed from them. Compatibility analysis in this research covers three checks: an Android-version check, comparing a device's reported API level against the application's extracted minimum SDK version; a required-feature check, comparing the device's reported supported hardware features against the application's declared `<uses-feature>` entries; and an ABI check, comparing the device's supported CPU architectures against the application's extracted native-library ABIs. Each of these checks produces one of three explicit outcomes, COMPATIBLE, INCOMPATIBLE, or NOT_AVAILABLE, with NOT_AVAILABLE returned whenever either the required application-side manifest fact or the required device-side data is missing, rather than a compatibility verdict being guessed in the absence of that data. Consistent with this principle, this research does not compute or report a RAM- or CPU-model-based compatibility requirement at all: no verified source of an application's required RAM or CPU model exists anywhere in the data available to this system, so this specific compatibility dimension is always reported as NOT_AVAILABLE rather than being fabricated from an unverified assumption.

## 3.17 Alternative Application Ranking Methodology

Where an assessed application is found to carry an elevated risk level, DualVulnDroid can suggest alternative, already-assessed applications from the same category as candidates the user might consider instead. The ranking methodology used for this feature is deliberately conservative and entirely dependent on real, previously stored assessment data. A candidate is excluded from consideration if it is the target application itself; if it does not have a real, stored risk assessment associated with it; or if its stored risk level is HIGH or CRITICAL. Category matching is applied only when the target application's own category is genuinely known from stored data; when the target's category is unknown, this filter is skipped entirely rather than being treated as either an automatic match or an automatic exclusion for every candidate. Surviving candidates are then ranked primarily by their real, stored risk level, lower severity ranked first, with ties broken by real, stored security score, higher score ranked first, and every returned candidate is accompanied by a specific, evidence-based reason for its inclusion, generated from the actual comparison performed, for example a real difference in stored risk level between the candidate and the target application. It is stated explicitly here that no popularity, user rating, or download-count metric is fabricated or otherwise invented by this methodology; such metrics are used only if they are genuinely stored and available for a given candidate, and none were available in the dataset underlying this research, so ranking here is based solely on the real, stored risk-assessment fields described above.

## 3.19 Evaluation and Validation Strategy

Evaluation and validation in this research was carried out at multiple, distinct levels, each targeting a different part of the system and each described in full, together with its results, in Chapter 9: unit testing of individual functions and classes across the backend, plugin, and Flutter codebases; integration testing of components that depend on one another, such as database persistence combined with the API layer; API-level testing of the FastAPI backend's HTTP endpoints; machine-learning evaluation of each classical model on the frozen test split described in Section 3.9; the research model comparison across all five evaluated models (three classical, two deep-learning); security-aware evaluation, applying the FPR/FNR-focused metrics described in Section 3.3 specifically to the vulnerability-detection task; robustness and sensitivity analysis of the Hybrid Decision Engine's threshold behaviour, described further in Section 3.21; backend validation through its full automated test suite; IntelliJ plugin testing through its Gradle-driven JUnit test suite; Flutter application testing through its own automated test suite; and, finally, real-environment validation, exercising the system in a genuine execution environment rather than a purely automated or simulated one, performed where the development environment available during this research actually permitted it.

The most recent verified test-suite totals recorded during this research were 316 backend tests passed with 2 skipped (the 2 skips corresponding to integration tests that require a separate MySQL test database connection not reachable in every environment this project was validated in, and reported as an honest skip rather than a fabricated pass), 55 Kotlin plugin tests passed, reflecting the addition of a set of tests that drive the plugin's real, registered analysis classes inside a genuine, headless IntelliJ Platform test fixture rather than testing isolated functions alone, and 39 Flutter tests passed. These totals are stated here specifically as evidence that a layered, automated testing strategy was actually executed, not as a single, unified test-suite result; each total comes from a structurally different test runner, a different stage of the project's development, and a different part of the system, and combining them into one number would misrepresent what was actually verified by each.

## 3.20 Ethical Considerations

Several ethical considerations were deliberately observed throughout this research. The labelled vulnerability dataset and the code samples it contains were used exclusively for defensive security research, specifically training and evaluating models intended to help identify and remediate CWE-89 and CWE-295, and not for any purpose intended to facilitate exploitation. Where real APKs were used to verify the manifest-extraction and compatibility pipeline, they were handled only for the purpose of extracting and validating the specific, real manifest metadata described in Section 3.16, and no attempt was made to reverse-engineer or redistribute any such application's proprietary content. Findings produced by the system, whether from the machine-learning model, the static/data-flow analysis, or the Hybrid Decision Engine, are never fabricated or presented with more certainty than the underlying evidence supports; where evidence is genuinely absent or inconclusive, the system is designed throughout to report that honestly, for example through the Decision Engine's SUSPICIOUS_REVIEW state and the compatibility module's NOT_AVAILABLE state, rather than to invent a confident-sounding answer.

Credentials and tokens are protected throughout the implemented system: passwords are never stored in plaintext and are hashed using Argon2 before persistence, authentication uses signed JSON Web Tokens rather than any weaker session mechanism, and error messages returned to a client are written to avoid leaking a token, password, or internal file-system path. No unnecessary personal data is collected from any user of the Flutter application beyond what is required for the account and assessment functionality actually described in this thesis. Where a real user account was needed to validate authentication behaviour, described further in Chapter 9, a dedicated test account was created and used specifically for that purpose, rather than reusing any pre-existing personal account. All model comparisons and Decision Engine configurations described in this research were conducted as controlled experiments on the frozen, versioned dataset split described in Section 3.9, and, consistent with the position stated throughout this thesis, no research-only threshold configuration, specifically the "Balanced" Decision Engine candidate discussed in Section 3.15, was deployed to the production system without the further validation this thesis's own limitations chapter states it would still require. Finally, this thesis reports limitations, failures, and unresolved gaps transparently wherever they were encountered during the research, rather than omitting them, consistent with the position that responsible security research should prioritise honest, defensive analysis over presenting an inflated account of a system's capabilities.

## 3.21 Reproducibility and Research Integrity

Reproducibility was treated as a first-class methodological requirement throughout this research, not an afterthought applied only at the point of writing this thesis. A fixed random seed (42) was used consistently across dataset splitting, model training, and deep-learning experiments; the train/validation/test split itself was frozen once constructed and reused, unchanged, across every model described in Sections 3.11 and 3.12, rather than being reconstructed separately for each model; and the artefacts produced at each stage, fitted vectorizers, trained model files, evaluation metrics, and split indices, were stored in a versioned, inspectable form rather than being discarded once a headline metric had been recorded. Hyperparameters selected for every evaluated model were recorded alongside that model's stored artefacts, and reproducibility metadata, covering software versions, the random seed, and split sizes, was recorded alongside the research evaluation results presented in later chapters. The vectorizers used for the production feature pipeline were, as described in Section 3.10, fit on the training split only, and the frozen test split was kept isolated from every hyperparameter- or threshold-selection decision throughout the research, consulted only once per model or configuration to produce a final, reported evaluation result. Where a test was genuinely unable to run in a given environment, for example the MySQL-dependent integration tests noted in Section 3.19, this was recorded as an explicit, honest skip rather than silently omitted or misrepresented as a pass. Provenance checks were applied to stored research artefacts before they were relied upon in this thesis, verifying that a given results file actually corresponded to the model and configuration it claimed to, rather than assuming this to be the case. Throughout this research, and consistently reflected in this thesis, a clear separation was maintained between the single production model, XGBoost, and the research-only models, SVM, Random Forest, LSTM, and CodeBERT, so that a result obtained from a research-only comparison is never presented as a description of production system behaviour.

One specific incident is recorded here as a concrete illustration of this research-integrity practice, rather than because it exposes an issue of ongoing concern to the reader of this thesis. During the consolidation of the CodeBERT research results, a provenance discrepancy was identified between a stored summary artefact and the model's actual, independently re-computed test-set evaluation. Rather than accept the summary figure at face value, the underlying, stored CodeBERT model artefact was re-evaluated directly against the frozen test split described in Section 3.9, and the results actually reported for CodeBERT throughout this thesis are drawn from that re-verified, direct re-evaluation rather than from the earlier, unverified summary file. This incident is included here specifically as an example of the artefact-verification practice applied throughout this research, checking a stored result against its underlying source before relying on it, rather than as a discussion of unrelated debugging detail.

## 3.22 Methodology Summary

This chapter has described the methodology followed throughout this research: an empirical, staged research design (Sections 3.2–3.5); controlled, leakage-checked dataset preparation and a fixed, group-aware train/validation/test split (Sections 3.6–3.9); a shared feature-engineering pipeline combining text-based and deterministic security features (Section 3.10); the training and validation-based selection of three classical machine-learning models and two supplementary deep-learning models, evaluated under an identical, fair comparison protocol (Sections 3.11–3.12); static and data-flow analysis used to complement, rather than replace, machine-learning prediction (Section 3.13); two methodologically distinct forms of explainability, SHAP-based model explanation and deterministic security evidence (Section 3.14); a Hybrid Decision Engine combining these sources of evidence into a three-state decision with an explicit coverage/reliability trade-off (Section 3.15); real, manifest-derived Android compatibility analysis and evidence-based alternative-application ranking (Sections 3.16–3.17); the actual tools and technologies used to implement the system (Section 3.18); a layered, multi-level evaluation and validation strategy (Section 3.19); and the ethical and reproducibility practices observed throughout the research (Sections 3.20–3.21). Together, these methodological choices were designed to support the five concerns stated at the outset of this chapter, reproducibility, leakage prevention, fair comparison, security-aware evaluation, and evidence-based conclusions, and they form the basis against which the system design, results, and conclusions presented in the remaining chapters of this thesis should be read. The following chapter, Chapter 4 — System Design and Architecture, builds directly on this methodology to describe the architecture of the DualVulnDroid system itself.
